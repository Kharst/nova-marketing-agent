How to Explain Solar Proposal Numbers to Clients

By Nova Metrics13 October 20264 min read

When a client looks at a solar proposal, the numbers can feel abstract. As an installer, translating those figures into a story of real savings and reliable performance is essential. This guide shows how the core calculations are built and which points to highlight so the client sees the value clearly. Nova Metrics’ Solar Intelligence platform structures these data, but the explanation works for any well‑prepared proposal.

Production Estimates – What Drives the Yield

The starting point is the system’s DC capacity in kilowatts. That figure is multiplied by South Africa’s standard specific yield of 1,700 kilowatt‑hours per kilowatt‑peak per year, a benchmark taken from the SAURAN Solar Atlas. From there, industry‑standard derates for losses such as soiling, wiring and inverter clipping are applied, followed by an availability factor that reflects typical downtime. The result is the annual energy production that the proposal quotes.

  • Specific yield: 1,700 kWh/kWp/yr (SAURAN Solar Atlas)
  • Derates for real‑world losses are built into the calculation
  • Availability factor accounts for expected downtime

Savings Calculation – Turning Production into Money

Year‑one savings are derived directly from the daily production estimate, not from a flat annual guess. The daily output is multiplied by the current tariff rate and then by the number of days in the year. From the second year onward, the model adds tariff escalation – 8.76 % for 2026 and 8.83 % for 2027, followed by roughly 5 % per year thereafter – because Eskom’s tariffs typically rise faster than panel performance degrades. Fixed grid charges are excluded from this figure, so they remain payable regardless of the system’s output.

  • Year‑one savings = daily production × tariff × 365
  • Tariff escalation: 8.76 % (2026), 8.83 % (2027), ~5 % thereafter
  • Grid charges are not part of the savings calculation

Financial Metrics for Commercial Clients

For business proposals, the platform runs a full 25‑year cash‑flow model. Each year’s net cash flow subtracts operating costs – maintenance at 1.25 % of capital, escalating at 5 % per year, and insurance at 0.75 % of capital – and adds any tax benefit. Battery replacement is scheduled for year ten at 70 % of the original battery cost. The cash flows are discounted at a nominal 12 % rate to produce net present value, and the internal rate of return is solved from the same series. Section 12B tax incentives are also factored: a 100 % first‑year write‑off for systems up to one megawatt, or a 50 %/30 %/20 % spread over three years for larger installations, plus VAT recovery in the first year.

  • 25‑year cash‑flow model
  • O&M: 1.25 % of capex, insurance: 0.75 % of capex
  • Battery replacement at year 10 (70 % of cost)
  • Discount rate: 12 %
  • Section 12B: 100 % write‑off ≤1 MW, tiered write‑off over 1 MW

Battery Utilisation – Matching Backup Needs

The proposal checks that the chosen battery can meet the client’s stated backup requirement, not just its nominal capacity. Depth of discharge is limited to 85 % for LiFePO₄ chemistry, and round‑trip efficiency is assumed at 92 %. The design assumes roughly 300 full cycles per year. If the calculated runtime falls short of 98 % of the client’s target autonomy hours, the system flags the sizing for review rather than passing silently. This check gives the client confidence that the backup will perform when needed.

  • DoD capped at 85 % for LiFePO₄
  • Round‑trip efficiency: 92 %
  • ~300 cycles per year
  • Runtime must meet 98 % of requested autonomy

Common questions

How is annual solar production calculated for a South African proposal?

It multiplies the system’s DC size by the specific yield of 1,700 kWh/kWp/yr, then applies standard loss derates and an availability factor to reflect real‑world performance.

What tariff growth rates are used to project savings after the first year?

The model applies an 8.76 % increase for 2026, 8.83 % for 2027, and an approximate 5 % rise each year thereafter, based on NERSA’s multi‑year price determination.

Which tax incentive is included for commercial solar projects up to one megawatt?

Section 12B provides a 100 % first‑year tax write‑off for systems of one megawatt or less, reducing the effective capital cost.
